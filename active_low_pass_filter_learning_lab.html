1. Introduction and learning objectives

A low-pass filter (LPF) is a frequency-selective circuit that passes signals below a chosen cutoff frequency and attenuates signals above it. In an active low-pass filter, an operational amplifier is used with resistors and capacitors to provide buffering and, when configured with feedback, voltage gain as well as frequency selection.

Why it matters

LPFs reduce high-frequency noise, limit signal bandwidth, smooth sensor signals and remove unwanted high-frequency components before sampling or further processing.

Prerequisites

Ohm’s law, impedance, capacitive reactance, phasors, op-amp ideal assumptions, negative feedback and Bode plots.

Learning outcomes

Identify passband/stopband, derive H(s), calculate fc, estimate gain in dB, and explain phase shift and practical limitations.

Key terminology

TermMeaning
PassbandFrequency range passed with relatively little attenuation.
StopbandFrequency range in which the signal is substantially attenuated.
Cutoff frequency, fcFor a first-order response, frequency at which magnitude is 1/√2 of passband gain (−3.01 dB).
Passband gain, AFLow-frequency closed-loop voltage gain of the non-inverting amplifier stage.
Roll-offAttenuation slope; first-order LPF approaches −20 dB/decade (−6 dB/octave).
OrderNumber of poles; a first-order LPF has one pole and one energy-storage capacitor.

Classification

  • Passive LPF: uses R, L and/or C; cannot provide power gain and may load adjacent stages.
  • Active LPF: uses an amplifier with R and C; can provide gain and buffering, but requires power supplies and has finite bandwidth.
  • By response/order: first-order, second-order and higher-order; common response families include Butterworth, Chebyshev, Bessel and elliptic.

3. Working principle

Step-by-step signal flow

  1. Input applied: Vin is applied through series resistor R to node V₁.
  2. Low frequency: Capacitor reactance XC=1/(2πfC) is large. Little current flows through C, so V₁ is close to Vin.
  3. High frequency: XC becomes small. More signal current is shunted through C to ground, reducing V₁.
  4. Amplification/buffering: The non-inverting op-amp amplifies V₁ by AF=1+Rf/Rg in the ideal closed-loop model.
  5. Output: Vout follows the low-pass-shaped signal, scaled by AF, while the op-amp remains in its linear region.

DC and AC behaviour

At DC (f = 0)

For steady state, an ideal capacitor is open circuit. The RC node is approximately equal to the input DC level. The op-amp output is AF times this level, provided it does not saturate.

At high frequency

The capacitor impedance decreases. The RC node tends toward AC ground, so the output magnitude decreases. The op-amp cannot restore frequencies removed by the RC network.

Operating assumptions

  • Ideal op-amp: very high input impedance, negligible input current, high open-loop gain, and operation with negative feedback.
  • Linear operation: output remains between the supply-dependent output swing limits.
  • Single-pole model: R and C are ideal and parasitic capacitances/stray inductances are ignored.
  • Practical op-amp bandwidth must be comfortably greater than the filter cutoff for the ideal response to be a good approximation.

Advantages and limitations

AdvantagesLimitations
Can provide voltage gain and buffering; high input impedance; low output impedance; convenient at low and moderate frequencies.Requires power supply; op-amp has finite gain-bandwidth product, slew rate, noise and output swing; component tolerances shift cutoff.
Simple design and easy simulation.A first-order filter has a gentle −20 dB/decade stopband slope; stronger rejection needs higher order.
Common misconception: Cutoff frequency is not a brick-wall boundary. A first-order filter is already attenuating at fc; at cutoff the amplitude is 70.7% of its passband value.

Magnitude and phase facts

  • Magnitude ratio: |H(jω)| = AF/√(1+(ωRC)²).
  • Phase: φ = −tan⁻¹(ωRC). It approaches 0° at low frequency and −90° at very high frequency.
  • At cutoff: magnitude = AF/√2 and phase = −45°.

5. Mathematical equations and derivation

Step 1: RC network transfer function

For a capacitor, ZC=1/(sC). By the voltage-divider relation:

V₁(s)/Vin(s) = ZC/(R + ZC) = 1/(1+sRC)

Step 2: Non-inverting op-amp gain

AF = 1 + Rf/Rg

Assuming the op-amp input does not load the RC network, Vout=AFV₁. Therefore:

H(s) = Vout(s)/Vin(s) = AF/(1+sRC)

Step 3: Cutoff frequency

Set s=jω. The magnitude is AF/√(1+(ωRC)²). At cutoff it equals AF/√2:

1 + (ωcRC)² = 2   ⇒   ωc = 1/RC
fc = 1/(2πRC)    and    τ = RC

Capacitive reactanceXC=1/(2πfC)Ohms; f in Hz, C in F.

At f = fc: |H|=0.707AF; gain is 3.01 dB below passband gain; phase is −45°. For f ≫ fc, attenuation approaches 20 dB per decade.

Design considerations

  • Choose R and C using fc=1/(2πRC). Very large R increases thermal-noise and bias-current error sensitivity; very small R loads the source.
  • Check op-amp gain-bandwidth product, input common-mode range, output swing, supply voltage and slew rate.
  • Use precision components when cutoff tolerance matters. For independent small tolerances, approximate relative cutoff error is Δfc/fc ≈ −(ΔR/R + ΔC/C).
  • For a higher-order filter, cascade stages carefully; stage loading and gain distribution can change the response.

6. Solved numerical problems

Example 1 · Basic

Find the cutoff frequency

Given: R=10 kΩ, C=10 nF. Find: fc.

Formula: fc=1/(2πRC).

Substitution: RC=(10×10³)(10×10⁻⁹)=100×10⁻⁶ s=100 μs.

fc = 1/(2π × 100 μs) ≈ 1.592 kHz

Interpretation: Signals near 1.592 kHz are 3.01 dB below the passband level.

Example 2 · Gain and output

Calculate passband gain and output at cutoff

Given: Rf=20 kΩ, Rg=10 kΩ, Vin=0.2 V peak, and input frequency is well below cutoff.

AF=1+20/10=3. In the passband, Vout≈3×0.2=0.6 V peak.

At cutoff, Vout peak = 0.6/√2 = 0.424 V.

AF=3;   Vout,fc≈0.424 V peak

Interpretation: Cutoff is defined relative to the passband output, not relative to the input.

Example 3 · GATE-style numerical

Find output magnitude at twice cutoff

Given: AF=2, fc=1 kHz, input=1 V rms, f=2 kHz. Find: output rms magnitude.

|H|=AF/√(1+(f/fc)²)=2/√5=0.8944.

Vout = 0.8944 × 1 V = 0.894 V rms

Interpretation: Output is less than the passband value (2 V rms) but not zero. Phase lag is −tan⁻¹(2)=−63.4°.

Useful shortcut: At f=10fc, the exact magnitude is AF/√101 ≈ 0.0995AF, close to one-tenth of passband gain (−20 dB). The asymptotic approximation is useful far above cutoff.

9. Applications and industry relevance

Sensor conditioning

Reduces high-frequency noise on temperature, pressure, light and other sensor outputs before ADC conversion.

Audio electronics

Acts as a tone-control or anti-noise stage, and limits bandwidth in audio signal paths. Audio crossover designs often use higher-order filters.

Data acquisition

Anti-aliasing filters attenuate frequency components above the useful band before sampling. The required cutoff depends on sampling rate and transition-band needs.

Instrumentation

Filters unwanted high-frequency interference in measurement and signal-conditioning circuits.

Industry design and validation workflow

  1. Translate signal bandwidth and noise requirements into a target cutoff and attenuation specification.
  2. Select filter order and response family based on passband flatness, phase response and transition steepness.
  3. Calculate nominal component values and check op-amp noise, bandwidth, swing, supply and loading.
  4. Simulate AC sweep, transient response and operating point; then prototype and measure with a signal generator and oscilloscope/network analyser.
  5. Compare measured cutoff and gain against tolerance limits; document deviations and verify across temperature and supply conditions where required.

Multisim simulation guide

  1. Place an op-amp, resistors, capacitor, ground and suitable DC supply rails.
  2. Wire the RC node to the non-inverting input. Connect Rf from output to the inverting input and Rg from the inverting input to ground.
  3. Set component values (for example R=10 kΩ, C=10 nF, Rf=Rg=10 kΩ) and choose an op-amp whose supply and bandwidth suit the test.
  4. Run an AC frequency sweep over a range spanning below and above the expected cutoff (about 1.59 kHz for the example RC values).
  5. Plot output/input magnitude in dB and phase. Confirm passband gain near 6.02 dB, −3.01 dB relative to passband at cutoff, and an eventual −20 dB/decade slope.
  6. Run transient analysis with a low-frequency sine and a frequency well above cutoff; compare input and output amplitudes and phase.

High-priority GATE and interview points

  • At cutoff, magnitude is 0.707 of passband gain, not 0.5.
  • At cutoff, phase shift for a first-order LPF is −45°.
  • Each first-order pole contributes −20 dB/decade asymptotic roll-off.
  • Increasing R or C decreases cutoff frequency; doubling either halves fc.
  • For voltage ratios use 20 log10; for power ratios use 10 log10.
  • The active stage can amplify in the passband, but it cannot recover frequencies attenuated by the RC network.

Memory aid

“Low goes, high is shunted.” At low frequency the capacitor is nearly open; at high frequency it offers a low impedance path to ground.
